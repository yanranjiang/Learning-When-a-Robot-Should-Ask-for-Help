Learning When a Robot Should Ask for Help:
Adaptive Intervention Requests in Human–Robot Teaming

Anonymous Authors

Under Review

PDFPaper▶Video+Supplementary

We learn a help-seeking policy that requests assistance when the robot is unlikely to recover autonomously and a human intervention is expected to be useful. The policy is evaluated with simulated users and in a physical user study.

Abstract

Robots operating in complex environments occasionally encounter situations where autonomous navigation fails. Timely human intervention can prevent or resolve these failures, but requesting help too frequently increases operator workload and reduces team efficiency. We propose an adaptive alert policy trained with offline reinforcement learning from past intervention outcomes and autonomous progress. The policy learns to request help selectively when an intervention is likely to be beneficial. We compare the learned policy with a threshold-based alert mechanism in simulation and in a physical user study. The results show improved exploration efficiency, fewer unnecessary alerts, and reduced operator workload without a significant change in trust.

Method

The policy receives a temporal window of scalar robot and interaction features together with a local occupancy grid. An MLP and GRU encode temporal observations, while a CNN encodes spatial context. The shared representation supports three heads:

  • Autonomy: estimates whether the robot can continue making progress independently.
  • Helpfulness: estimates the observed outcome likely to follow a human waypoint intervention.
  • Action value: selects between autonomous continuation and requesting assistance.

The binary policy is trained offline using Conservative Q-Learning. The auxiliary objectives regularize the shared representation and provide task-relevant learning signals.

The learned action is only the robot’s help-seeking decision: continue autonomously (at = 0) or issue an assistance request (at = 1). A human waypoint ut is an external response, not an action selected by the policy. After receiving that waypoint, planning and execution remain autonomous. The interaction order is st → ot → at → ut → st+1.

Experimental Setup

Simulation

The policy is trained on 134 semi-autonomous navigation episodes. Evaluation uses expertise-conditioned intervention models learned from data collected from 27 participants. These models generate 50 novice-derived and 50 expert-derived stochastic interaction rollouts; they are not additional independent human participants.

Physical user study

Twenty participants completed three randomized ten-minute exploration trials with no alerts, threshold alerts, and learned adaptive alerts. Participants supervised a Boston Dynamics Spot robot while concurrently performing an object-verification task.

Architecture and training details

A 100-step observation history addresses partial observability. Scalar observations are encoded by a two-layer MLP and a 256-unit GRU. A residual convolutional encoder maps the 23 × 23 × 3 local costmap to a 256-dimensional spatial embedding. Their concatenation forms the shared 512-dimensional representation. The auxiliary predictions are parallel training heads; they are not fed directly into the Q-value head.

Simulated intervention models

Intervention data from 27 prior-study participants were divided into novice- and expert-derived groups; intermediate operators were assigned using their prior task performance rather than discarded. A separate gradient-boosted multi-output regressor was trained for each group (100 estimators, learning rate 0.05, maximum depth 3, squared-error loss). Each rollout samples a waypoint-distance parameter from the corresponding empirical group distribution and retains it throughout the rollout, providing a limited persistent behavioral characteristic. The resulting 50 novice-derived and 50 expert-derived rollouts are controlled stochastic instances—not 100 independent human participants and not a model of the full diversity of human behavior.

Baselines and alert-level metric

The threshold policy issues an alert after displacement below 0.04 m for 12 s or no increase in explored area for 10 s, with thresholds tuned on validation episodes disjoint from the evaluation maps. The score-based policy alerts when predicted autonomy is below 0.2 and predicted intervention outcome is above 0.2; both thresholds were fixed using held-out validation data. LinUCB uses the same logged actions, reward, and observable context as the proposed policy. Validation selected exploration coefficient α = 0.5 and ridge coefficient λ = 1.0; its parameters remain fixed during closed-loop evaluation.

Progress per alert is a mission-level attention-efficiency measure, not progress caused by an individual intervention. Alert-level effectiveness is measured as newly explored area in a fixed 10 s window: Pi(10) = E(ti + 10 s) − E(ti).

Physical failure landscape

Intervention-free runs identified 12 recurrent failure-prone locations before the study: two physical immobilization events, three planner/navigation deadlocks, four goal/frontier oscillations, and three sustained low-progress regions. The map, barriers, autonomy stack, mission duration, and failure locations were held fixed across conditions.

Why waypoint intervention is needed

The policy operates above a deployed autonomy stack comprising 3D LiDAR SLAM, global exploration planning, and search-based local navigation with obstacle avoidance and replanning. It does not replace those modules. Assistance is reserved for states outside their normal recovery envelope—for example, corrupted pose or map estimates, spurious costmap obstacles, constrained geometric deadlocks, or repeated frontier switching. A waypoint changes the high-level objective; subsequent planning and execution remain autonomous.

Limitations

  • The helpfulness target is an observational intervention-outcome label rather than a causal estimate.
  • Simulation assumes immediate alert responses, while real users may delay or ignore them.
  • The physical study contains 20 participants. Age and gender were not collected, limiting demographic characterization and generalizability; only prior robot-supervision and navigation-game experience frequencies were recorded.
  • The simulated rollouts provide limited expertise-conditioned variation and should not be interpreted as independent human participants.
  • Evaluation is limited to one exploration task, robot platform, and physical environment.
  • The binary action space addresses when to request waypoint assistance, not which type of assistance to request.
